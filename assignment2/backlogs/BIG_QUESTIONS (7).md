## Q1 — Does direct bus-level forecasting outperform zone-forecast + top-down disaggregation?

**Why it matters:** This is the central architectural question. Triebe et al. (L5) showed on MISO data that top-down disaggregation achieves MASE=2.06 (twice worse than naive) while direct bus forecasting achieves MASE=0.86. Does the same pattern hold on ERCOT data at 15,000-bus scale?

**Published benchmark for our expected effect size (added after L5 anchor-tier rewrite from PDF):** L5 Table 3 gives us four specific reduction-vs-top-down numbers that we can compare our ERCOT result against directly. On Utility A's ~100 buses, Global-Bus (direct bus model with bottom-up reconciliation) achieves bus-level RMSE 0.58, MAE 0.38, MASE 0.86, MSSE 0.67 — versus Local-Utility (top-down disaggregation) at bus-level RMSE 7.88, MAE 7.79, MASE 2.06, MSSE 4.04. This is a **93% reduction in RMSE, 95% reduction in MAE, 58% reduction in MASE, and 83% reduction in MSSE** when going from top-down to direct bus modeling at bus level — the headline result from L5's published Conclusion (Section 7, finding III: "Our system improves bus-level accuracy substantially, reducing the error by 33% (MSSE) and 14% (MASE) compared to sNaïve, and 93% (RMSE) and 95% (MAE) compared to a top-down disaggregation"). The L5 numbers are our **publication-grade reference**: if our ERCOT result on 15,000 buses shows the same direction with similar magnitudes (say >50% RMSE reduction direct-vs-top-down), this is a quantitative replication of the L5 anchor finding on a fundamentally larger system.

**How to answer it:** Compare (a) direct bus LightGBM predictions against (b) zone LightGBM × historical bus share. Evaluate both at bus level using MAE, RMSE, WMAPE. The gap between them is the value of learning individual bus history.

**Method specification (added after L5 re-read):** The historical bus share for the top-down disaggregation must be computed on training years only (2022–2024), not the full dataset. Computing share on 2025 data would constitute label leakage. Share = mean(bus_pd) / mean(zone_pd) over the training window, with the mean computed over non-null hours only.

**Framing on disaggregation weight choice (added after L23, L25 review; extended after L30, L34 review):** Our Q1 baseline uses *historical share* as the disaggregation weight; published top-down work commonly uses *population share* (e.g., L25 Xu et al. 2020 disaggregates US BAA-level load to buses by ZIP-code population; L23 Unlu & Peña 2025 disaggregates ISO-NE zonal load to IEEE buses by static IEEE base-case share), *recent historical share* (L30 Sun et al. 2013 disaggregates zonal load to substations by Bus Load Distribution Factor — last-3-weeks average ratio of substation load to zone load at the same weekday-hour), or *power-flow-derived sensitivity weights* (L34 Schröter et al. 2020 disaggregates German DSO node injections to TSO substations via 1 MW power-flow simulation and Newton-Raphson sensitivity factors aᵢ,ₖ). All four approaches are static-weight disaggregations in the sense that the disaggregation operator is computed offline from prior data (or a network model) and applied as a multiplicative factor to the zonal forecast. The L5 (Triebe et al.) anchor result that direct beats top-down is expected to generalize across these weight choices, since all of them reduce to multiplying a zonal forecast by a time-invariant scalar at forecast time. We use historical share because we have it; L25 used population because they did not have real bus-level load; L30 used BLDF as an architectural choice for routing only the well-behaved substations through the cheap path; L34 used power-flow sensitivities because they had a calibrated 110 kV network model. Citing L25, L23, L30, and L34 alongside L5 in the methodology paragraph strengthens the top-down baseline framing across multiple weight-choice variants (data-driven historical, simulation-driven power-flow, and demographic-proxy population).

**What we already know:** L5 gives us strong prior expectation that (a) wins substantially. Demonstrating this on ERCOT at 15,000-bus scale is a citable finding even if the magnitude differs.

**Required models:** Zone model + share disaggregation (top-down baseline), Direct global bus model.

## Q3a — Does forecast accuracy degrade in FWES and NOTH due to structural load growth?

**Why it matters:** This is the most ERCOT-specific challenge. Neither L5 (Triebe et al.) nor L4 (Salgado et al.) dealt with 60% load growth in a single zone over 3 years. A model trained on 2022–2024 averages and predicting 2025 will systematically underpredict FWES unless it captures the trend explicitly. This is also our out-of-distribution test in the L15 (LIPS) multi-dimensional evaluation sense — FWES/NOTH 2025 is genuinely outside the training distribution.

**How to answer it (descriptive — primary):** Report per-zone WMAPE in the evaluation. Compare FWES and NOTH error against the other 6 zones. If FWES/NOTH errors are substantially higher, the model has not fully learned the structural growth. If they are comparable, the trend feature worked.

**Required models:** Direct bus model with year/trend feature included. No additional model needed — just stratified evaluation by zone.

**What we already know from data (E-series):** FWES and NOTH load growth confirmed from C1. Same-hour-last-year baseline will be worst in these zones. This is the most important stratified error analysis to report.

**Framing context (added after L22 review):** L22 (Cheng & Chen 2025) empirically documents that population distribution and zone-level load are ~90% correlated under normal conditions (cosine 0.8959, Pearson 0.9080 on Alberta planning areas). This is the *control case*. Our ERCOT FWES/NOTH growth is the *anomaly* — FWES grew 60% and NOTH grew 51% from 2022–2025, but the population in West Texas and the Panhandle did not grow at anything close to those rates. The growth is industrial (data centers, crypto, Permian Basin, Lubbock Power & Light integration), not demographic. This frames our structural growth challenge as a *departure from the strong population-correlated baseline that L22 documents*. A model relying only on population-correlated geographic features will fail in FWES and NOTH for precisely this reason. The trend or year feature is needed because population alone cannot capture industrial load growth.

**Framing tightening (added after L24 review):** L24 (Safdarian et al. 2023) complements L22's population finding with the temperature dimension. Using 12 years of ERCOT public load data on the same eight weather zones we have in our dataset, L24 documents that Far West (= FWES) has near-zero temperature-load correlation across all four temperature partitions (peak |r|=0.17), while all other seven zones show |r|>0.80 above 80°F and |r|>0.65 below 60°F. The authors attribute Far West's weather insensitivity to dominant industrial load (Permian Basin oil/gas). Combining L22 + L24 produces a tight argument: FWES growth is uncorrelated with both **population** (L22, the normal demographic baseline) and **temperature** (L24, the normal weather-sensitivity baseline). The growth is structural-industrial. Two key implications for our methodology: (i) the trend/year feature is the only available mechanism in our pipeline to capture this growth, since population and temperature features would not have predicted it anyway; (ii) our deliberate choice not to use weather features is not structurally disadvantaged in FWES — temperature is not predictive there. In residential/commercial-dominated zones (Coast, North, South, etc.), the absence of weather features is a real but bounded methodological limitation we should acknowledge honestly in the report.

**Substation-scale corroboration (added after L30 review):** L30-B (Sun et al. 2013) documents at substation scale on a New Zealand grid that a single industrial-dominated substation (A15, "very close to a major industry geographically") has MAPE 14.23% — substantially higher than residential/mixed substations on the same grid. **This is independent corroboration of L24-A at a different scale (substation, not zone), in a different country (New Zealand, not Texas), with a different consumer mix (single dominant industrial customer, not Permian Basin oil/gas aggregate).** The pattern of {industrial dominance → unobservable demand drivers → high forecast error} appears robust across geographies and aggregation scales. For our Q3a × Q4 narrative, this means we should expect *both* zone-level error in FWES to be high (Q3a) *and* individual industrial buses within FWES to be the hardest buses to forecast at the bus level (Q4). The two findings compound rather than duplicate.

## Q4 — At what spatial resolution does forecast accuracy degrade, and is bus-level forecasting even reliable for small buses?

**Why it matters:** **L29 (Sevlian & Rajagopal 2018, IJEPES — direct assessment)** is the canonical published reference for aggregation effects on load forecasting accuracy. The paper develops a two-parameter empirical scaling law CV(W) = α₀/Wᵖ + α₁ with p ≈ 1, validated across >180,000 PG&E residential and SMB customers, five forecast methods (SARMA, SVR, FFNN), and two climate sub-populations. The law identifies two regimes: a **scaling regime** where relative error decreases roughly as 1/W with aggregation, and a **saturation regime** beyond a method-specific **critical load W★** where no further improvement is possible. For hour-ahead forecasting in L29's PG&E data, W★ varies from ~2.2 MWh (worst model) to ~72 MWh (best model). Many of our ERCOT buses have mean pd well below 10 MW — for our LightGBM forecaster they likely sit in the scaling regime, meaning bus-size matters substantially for relative error. **The L5 paraphrase ("accuracy degrades steeply below 1 MW") is correct in spirit but loose in detail** — the actual transition is model-dependent and forecast-horizon-dependent, sitting in the range 0.1–10 MWh for hour-ahead and even lower for day-ahead (L29-C: day-ahead M6 has W★ ≈ 85 kWh).

**How to answer it:** Stratify bus-level WMAPE by bus size (mean pd across training period). **Plot per-bus WMAPE against bus mean pd on a log-log scale.** If Q1 (smallest buses) shows dramatically higher error than Q4 (largest buses), the scaling regime is confirmed on ERCOT data. **Then fit L29's scaling law form WMAPE(W) = α₀/Wᵖ + α₁ and report:**
- The exponent p (expected range 0.88–1.07 from L29's range across methods)
- The reducible error α₀ (expected ~50 from L29 — if dramatically different, that itself is reportable)
- The irreducible error α₁ (the diagnostic quantity for our LightGBM model's performance — directly comparable to L29's α₁ = 1.19–2.13 across SARMA/SVR/FFNN models)
- The critical load W★ (the bus-size threshold where aggregation stops helping for our model)

If our LightGBM at ERCOT-bus-scale produces a clean fit to L29's functional form, we have replicated the scaling law on a fundamentally different (transmission-bus) domain. If the fit is poor, the deviation is itself a finding worth reporting.

**Scaling axis note (L29-E):** Use mean pd in MWh as the x-axis, not bus count. L29 Section 5.4 demonstrates that residential and SMB customer types follow the same scaling law when scaled by kWh despite differing by 9× in per-customer consumption — kWh (or MWh) is the canonical scaling quantity, not number of customers or buses.

**Forecast horizon note (L29-C):** Our forecast horizon is next-day-ahead at hourly resolution. L29's hour-ahead and day-ahead results are both relevant; report W★ with the horizon explicitly stated. Don't conflate our results with L29's hour-ahead numbers if the comparison is to day-ahead.

**Preprocessing note:** "Mean pd across training period" is computed over non-null hours only, on the 2022–2024 training window. The 2,137 always-null buses are excluded entirely (no mean to compute). For the 4,425 buses that switch LOAD↔ISOLATED, the mean is computed only over LOAD-state hours. The 269 mixed-null buses use mean over observed hours.

**Stratification refinement (optional):** L29 finds the scaling law is robust across customer-type subpopulations (residential vs. SMB) and climate subpopulations (coastal vs. inland) when scaled by mean kWh. As a refinement, we could fit the scaling law separately for our Q6-derived cluster groups (residential-shape vs. commercial-shape vs. industrial-shape buses). If the three sector-clusters produce different α₁ values, that's evidence that sector composition affects the forecastability ceiling — a methodologically interesting finding linking Q4 to Q6.

**Cross-country, cross-grid-type corroboration (added after L31 review):** L31 (Haben et al. 2021, *Applied Energy* review) cites Haben et al. (2019) finding the same power-law relationship between feeder size and relative forecast error on UK low-voltage distribution feeders that L29 documented on PG&E residential and SMB customers. **The scaling law is therefore not specific to PG&E or to California — it has been replicated by an independent author group on a different country's grid at a different aggregation level (UK LV-feeder vs US distribution).** **Added after L40 review:** L40 (Pinheiro et al. 2023, *Applied Energy*, Portuguese DSO) provides a third independent qualitative confirmation: with the same GAM-M3 methodology held constant across 96,989 secondary substations, single-customer-aggregated PTC substations (median MAPE ~40%+, only 66.0% beat naive day-ahead) forecast substantially worse than neighborhood-aggregated PTD substations (median MAPE 12.6%, 82.8% beat naive). L40 does not fit a scaling law explicitly but the PTD-vs-PTC contrast is empirically consistent with L29's prediction that smaller-aggregation cases have higher relative error. **For our Q4, three independent confirmations across three countries (US-PG&E, UK-LV-feeders, Portugal-secondary-substations) and three author groups dramatically strengthens the citation strength.** Any reviewer questioning whether L29's result generalizes to our ERCOT transmission-bus data can be pointed to L29 + L31 + L40 jointly. If our LightGBM-at-ERCOT result fits the same functional form, we are adding a fourth independent confirmation — a fundamentally reportable result regardless of the specific α₀, α₁, W★ values we obtain.

**Required models:** Direct bus model. No additional model needed — stratified evaluation + log-log scaling-law fit (~10 lines of scipy.optimize.curve_fit code).

## Q5 — Does the zone model evaluated directly at zone level outperform the zone implied by summing bus-level predictions?

**Why it matters:** This tests whether building a dedicated zone model adds value over just summing individual bus forecasts. It also tests whether the bus-level model is internally consistent — if summed bus predictions are close to zone predictions, the model has learned something physically coherent.

**How to answer it:** Compare (a) zone LightGBM predictions directly against zone_load_2025.parquet, vs (b) sum of bus-level predictions against zone_load_2025.parquet. If (a) is substantially better, the zone model captures zone-level dynamics that bus-level pooling misses. If (b) matches (a), direct bus forecasting is sufficient and the zone model is redundant.

**Interpretation caveat:** A match between (a) and (b) is evidence of internal consistency but not evidence of correctness. Both could be making the same systematic error (e.g., both underpredicting FWES due to growth). Always interpret Q5 results alongside Q3a's per-zone error breakdown.

**Framing support (added after L27 review):** L27 (Li et al. 2021, IEEE TPS) demonstrates by construction that summing bus-level synthetic profiles to system level matches actual system-level load on the ERCOT-footprint ACTIVSg2000 system (Figure 9: synthetic peak 71.1 GW vs. actual ERCOT 2016 peak 71.2 GW). This is the closest published precedent for Q5's hypothesis: bus-level profiles, when correctly modeled, aggregate coherently to zone/system level. If our bus-level predictions sum to within a few percent of the zone-level model's predictions, this is consistent with L27's finding that bus-level decomposition can be made internally consistent with the aggregate. If the two diverge substantially, that is evidence either of bus-level modeling error or of structural misallocation between zone and bus.

**Framing support (added after L29 review):** L29 (Sevlian & Rajagopal 2018) provides the theoretical lens for interpreting Q5 results. The scaling law CV(W) = α₀/Wᵖ + α₁ predicts that relative error at *zone-level aggregation* should be substantially smaller than at *bus-level aggregation* — because zone-level mean pd is much larger and the zone is firmly in the saturation regime where α₁ dominates, whereas individual buses are in the scaling regime where α₀/W still contributes meaningfully. Therefore: a direct zone model should outperform bus-summed predictions in relative terms not because the zone model is "better" architecturally, but because zone-level aggregation has intrinsically lower relative error per L29. If our bus-summed predictions match the direct zone model's accuracy, this is **surprisingly good** — it means bus-level residuals are well-correlated and cancel on aggregation, recovering zone-level forecastability from bus-level work. If the bus-summed predictions are substantially worse than the direct zone model in relative terms, that is the *expected* result per L29 and tells us nothing surprising — it's the price of working at higher spatial resolution.

**Mechanism for bus-summed coherence (added after L30 review; second citation added after L34 review; third citation added after L40 review; fourth citation added after L5 anchor-tier rewrite):** L30-A (Sun et al. 2013) documents an empirical mechanism by which bus-summed predictions can be *more* accurate than the sum of individual-bus accuracies would predict. Sun et al. observe two adjacent substations (A9 and A11) with individual MAPEs of 6.05% and 10.34%, but their summed load achieves MAPE 3.71% — substantially better than either individually. The authors attribute this to load switching between the substations through network reconfiguration that is not observable from time-series alone: physical conservation forces the sum to be more stable than the individual time series. **L34-D (Schröter et al. 2020) corroborates the same mechanism from a different TSO context** — the German 50Hertz operational paper explicitly identifies that "switching operations in the subordinate network from time to time that are not communicated to the transmission system operator [...] lead to a shift in the generation between substations" as a documented source of substation-level forecast error (Section 4.1.2). **L40-D (Pinheiro et al. 2023) adds a third independent citation in a different direction — the aggregation-improves-forecastability mechanism.** At Portuguese DSO scale, holding the GAM-M3 methodology constant across 96,989 secondary substations, PTC substations (single large customer) have median MAPE ~40%+ with only 66.0% beating naive day-ahead, while PTD substations (DSO-owned, aggregating dozens to hundreds of low-voltage clients per neighborhood) achieve median MAPE 12.6% with 82.8% beating naive. **Same model, same pipeline, only aggregation level differs** — direct empirical evidence that aggregating across heterogeneous consumers averages out idiosyncratic noise and improves forecastability. This is a *different* mechanism than L30-A/L34-D (which describe network-reconfiguration creating anticorrelated errors); L40-D describes consumer-heterogeneity averaging. Both mechanisms operate in the same direction (aggregation improves predictability) but through distinct physical causes. **L5 (Triebe et al. 2025) adds a fourth independent citation — direct head-to-head measurement of bus-summed-vs-zone-direct at utility-aggregate scale.** L5 Table 2 reports utility-level forecast accuracy on aggregated bus load for Utility A: Local-Utility (top-down direct utility forecast disaggregated to buses) achieves utility-aggregate RMSE 18.68, MAE 13.09, MASE 0.96, MSSE 0.87; **Global-Bus (bottom-up sum of individual bus forecasts) achieves RMSE 18.70, MAE 12.75, MASE 0.72, MSSE 0.62 — matching Local-Utility on absolute metrics and substantially beating on scaled metrics**; Grouped Global-Bus (bottom-up sum from three cluster-specific models) achieves RMSE 17.72, MAE 11.92, MASE 0.68, MSSE 0.55 — the best across all metrics. **The headline empirical result for our Q5: at utility aggregate scale, bottom-up sum of bus forecasts is competitive with or better than top-down direct utility forecast.** Four peer-reviewed sources from four different countries (US/UConn 2013, Germany/50Hertz 2020, Portugal/EDP 2023, US/MISO 2025) now identify mechanisms or direct measurements producing bus/substation-summed coherence at scale. For our 4,425 LOAD↔ISOLATED-switching buses (D3 in primary backlog), L30-A/L34-D's network-reconfiguration mechanism applies; for buses with heterogeneous downstream consumer mix, L40-D's averaging mechanism applies; L5 Table 2 provides the strongest single piece of empirical evidence that the *outcome* (bus-summed matching or beating zone-direct on scaled metrics) actually occurs on real TSO data. **This now provides a four-citation-anchor mechanism stack for why bus-summed predictions could plausibly match or exceed zone-direct predictions even in the scaling regime that L29 predicts should disadvantage bus-summed predictions.** If Q5's empirical result shows bus-summed matching zone-direct, L30-A + L34-D + L40-D + L5 are the citations for explaining why physically and empirically. If bus-summed is substantially worse, both mechanisms are muted or absent in our data and the L29 scaling-law explanation prevails.

**Required models:** Zone model, Direct bus model (evaluated at zone level after summation via E3 aggregation check).

## Q6 — Does a bus's sector type (inferred from diurnal shape) carry predictive information?

**Why it matters:** L27 (Li et al. 2021, IEEE TPS) is the primary methodological anchor — peer-reviewed in the flagship power-systems journal, on the same ERCOT-footprint synthetic grid (ACTIVSg2000, eight weather zones) we are working with. L27-A documents that residential, commercial, and industrial buses have visually distinct diurnal signatures on the ERCOT footprint, and L27-B confirms that these are recoverable from normalized 24-hour shape vectors. L4 (Salgado et al. 2011) provides the methodological foundation (normalize before clustering, then k-means by shape). **L28 (Panapakidis et al. 2020, IET GTD) provides the strongest single piece of peer-reviewed empirical evidence that cluster-then-train improves bus load forecasting accuracy over no-clustering baselines** — L28-A shows that across four Greek island distribution buses, three clustering algorithms (FCM, K-means, Ward's), and three input feature sets, the cluster + FFNN hybrid produces lower MAPE than the same FFNN with no clustering (Table 15 of L28). L28's architecture is different from ours (cluster-then-separate-models per bus, vs. our cluster-as-feature in a global model), but the central finding — that bus-level clustering carries predictive information — transfers. **L31-C (Haben et al. 2021, *Applied Energy* review) provides further independent endorsement** of the sector-type-matters principle at LV-feeder scale — Haben et al. document that LV feeders with high proportions of overnight-storage-heater customers behave dramatically differently from feeders with other customer mixes, validating that customer-mix is a critical forecasting input. The combined anchor stack for Q6 — L4 (foundation) + L27 (ERCOT footprint) + L28 (empirical ablation) + L31-C (independent LV-level endorsement) — is now a four-piece evidence cluster spanning different scales and grid types. If sector type is informative, including it as a feature in the global bus model should reduce error, particularly at hours where sector types diverge most (overnight for industrial vs. residential, midday for commercial). L2 (Li et al. 2018 TPEC conference) is the precursor to L27 and is no longer cited as the primary anchor.

**How to answer it:** Compute normalized average diurnal shape per bus from 2022–2024 training data. Run k-means (k=3–5) to assign each bus a cluster label. Include cluster label as a categorical feature in the global LightGBM bus model. Compare WMAPE with and without the cluster feature (ablation), not just feature importance — feature importance alone is a ranking without a calibrated effect size.

**Expected cluster proportions (sanity check, added after L27 review):** L27-A documents that on the ACTIVSg2000 ERCOT-footprint synthetic grid (1,125 load buses, 2016 EIA sector composition), **67.4% of buses are residential-dominated, 18.8% are commercial-dominated, and 13.8% are industrial-dominated**. This is the expected baseline if our 2022–2025 ERCOT bus-level data has a similar sector composition to 2016. If our clustering produces roughly 60–70% residential-shape / 15–25% commercial-shape / 10–20% industrial-shape clusters, this validates the clustering against the L27 baseline. **Reportable finding to watch for:** if our 2022–2025 clustering produces noticeably *more* industrial-shape buses than the 2016 baseline of 14%, this is evidence of ERCOT's recent industrial expansion (FWES data centers/crypto/Permian, NOTH growth) — ties Q6 directly to Q3a's structural growth narrative.

**Similarity metric choice (refined after L18, L19, L20, L28 reviews):** Three options for the clustering similarity metric, in order of methodological strength:
- **Baseline (current plan):** K-means on Euclidean distance over normalized 24-hour diurnal shape vectors. Defensible and simple. Cite L4 (Salgado et al.), L27 (Li et al. 2021), and **L28-B (Panapakidis et al. 2020 explicitly finds K-means outperforms FCM and Ward's on bus load forecasting; clustering algorithm choice matters more than NN training algorithm or activation function)** as anchors for the K-means choice.
- **Upgrade option 1 — PCC clustering (L18-A, L19-A):** K-means on Pearson correlation matrix rather than Euclidean distance. Fully scale-invariant. Two independent papers (L18 in EV charging, L19 in AMI) support this as superior to Euclidean.
- **Upgrade option 2 — Mask-aware Euclidean (L20-A):** Standard Euclidean modified to compute distance only over coordinates where both buses have observed values. Relevant for the 269 mixed-null buses where interpolation might distort the diurnal shape.

Recommendation: use baseline Euclidean for the primary result; if time permits, run upgrade option 1 (PCC) as a robustness check and report whether cluster assignments are stable across the two metrics. Do not implement option 2 unless mixed-null bus clustering proves problematic.

**Cluster shape interpretation caveat (added after L27 review):** L27-D notes that residential customer-level diurnal shapes can shift by up to ±12 hours (calibrated against real Pecan Street smart-meter data). At the bus level, this customer-level diversity is averaged out, so cluster labels reflect the *dominant aggregate* behavior of the bus, not homogeneous customer composition. When reporting Q6 results, describe cluster shapes as aggregates of underlying diversity ("commercial-dominated buses" rather than "commercial buses").

**What we already know:** We have 3+ years of hourly pd per bus. Computing normalized diurnal shapes and running k-means is straightforward. The 2,137 always-null buses and ISOLATED buses are excluded before clustering. We expect to see roughly 3 natural clusters corresponding to industrial (flat), residential (morning + evening peaks), and commercial/mixed (daytime peak), with approximate proportions matching L27-A's ~67/19/14 split.

**Feasibility:** Low additional effort if done as a feature engineering step in notebook 02. The cluster label computation adds one preprocessing step. The model comparison is a with/without-cluster ablation on the same LightGBM model.

**Scope boundary:** Training a separate model per cluster (Grouped Global-Bus from L5 Triebe et al.) is a future option in the literature backlog, not a required experiment here. The question here is only whether the cluster label is informative as a feature, not whether it justifies separate models.

**Required models:** Direct global bus model with cluster label, Direct global bus model without cluster label (ablation).

## Q7 — Does the choice of imputation method for the 269 mixed-null buses affect downstream forecast accuracy?

**Why it matters:** The M2 preprocessing decision specifies linear interpolation for the 269 mixed-null LOAD buses with contiguous null blocks. L20-B (Zhao et al. 2024) provides direct empirical support for this choice: in their AGCIN paper, the local-only imputation (GCIN, equivalent to interpolation-class methods) is competitive with full GAN-based global imputation specifically in segment-missing scenarios. Our 269 buses exhibit segment missingness (null blocks of 10–552 hours per M2 confirmation), not random missingness. This means linear interpolation is the *theoretically supported* choice, not a corner-cutting compromise. But this needs empirical confirmation on our data.

**How to answer it:** On a sample of 50 mixed-null buses, run the direct bus model under three imputation regimes for the training period: (a) linear interpolation between adjacent hours (M2 primary plan), (b) forward-fill from last observed value, (c) zone-mean fill (replace null with the zone's mean pd at that hour). Compare WMAPE on the 2025 test period for these 50 buses. If WMAPE is within ~5% across methods, the choice doesn't matter and L20-B is empirically confirmed on our data. If linear interpolation is substantially better, M2 is justified. If something else wins, M2 needs revision.

**Why a sample, not all 269:** Training the full bus model three times is expensive. A 50-bus subsample stratified by null fraction (10–50 hours, 50–200 hours, 200–552 hours) gives sufficient statistical power to detect a meaningful difference between methods without rerunning the full pipeline three times.

**What we already know:** L20-B is the strongest single piece of evidence. L7 (Türkoğlu et al. Q7 lets us defend that choice empirically rather than just by citation.

**Additional candidate methods from anchor-tier rewrites (added after L5, L12 anchor-tier rewrite from PDFs):** Two more imputation precedents are now available from the literature:
- **L5-F (Triebe et al. 2025 preprocessing recipe):** linear interpolation up to 20 consecutive missing entries, then rolling-mean imputation for longer null blocks. This is a *hybrid* approach — interpolation as primary method, rolling-mean as fallback for extended gaps. Direct precedent from the methodological anchor paper on a closely-related TSO problem (~100 MISO buses with similar 0.16-37 MWh load range). **For our segment-missing buses with very long null blocks (200–552 hours), a rolling-mean fallback may outperform pure linear interpolation, which can produce visibly artificial straight-line segments over long gaps.** Worth adding as a fourth candidate method (d) if compute permits, or as a tweak to method (a): hybrid linear+rolling-mean per L5-F.

From L12 Section 2.2: "KNN is well-suited for time-series data with recurrent patterns as it imputes values based on the similarity to neighboring observations, thereby preserving the inherent structure of the dataset." ## Q8 — Does the L29 scaling law hold on our ERCOT bus data, and if so, what are α₀, α₁, p, and W★?

**Why it matters:** Q4 is framed as a *diagnostic* question — "does accuracy degrade at small buses?" — and answers it through stratified WMAPE by size quartile. Q8 promotes the same data to a *quantitative-fit* question: the L29 scaling law `WMAPE(W) = α₀/Wᵖ + α₁` is a published two-parameter empirical relationship that has been independently confirmed on three different grids (L29 PG&E residential/SMB, L31 Haben et al. 2019 UK LV-feeders, L40 Pinheiro et al. 2023 Portuguese PTC/PTD substations) across three countries and three author groups. **If our LightGBM at ERCOT-bus-scale produces a clean fit to this functional form, we are adding a fourth independent confirmation across a fundamentally different domain — US transmission-bus scale, on a modern gradient-boosted-tree forecaster.** This is a top-line reportable result regardless of the specific parameter values obtained, because the literature has not yet documented whether the scaling law extends to transmission-bus scale on a real grid. **The asymmetry of outcomes makes this a "free hit":**
- If the fit is clean: we add an independent confirmation of a well-established scaling law in a new domain (publishable in its own right, and a defense of our methodology being statistically well-behaved).
- If the fit is poor: the deviation is itself diagnostic — it tells us whether transmission-bus dynamics differ structurally from the distribution-feeder and substation regimes the law has been confirmed in.

Either way, the result is reportable. **This is a question whose existence is forced by the literature** — we cannot honestly report Q4's stratified-WMAPE result without also reporting the scaling-law fit, because Q4's stratification *is* an unfitted version of the scaling law.

**How to answer it:** After Q4's per-bus-size-quartile stratification is complete:
1. Compute per-bus mean pd over the training window (2022–2024 LOAD-state hours only, excluding always-null and ISOLATED-state hours per the L29 preprocessing convention).
2. Compute per-bus WMAPE on the 2025 test window from the direct bus model's predictions.
3. Fit `WMAPE(W) = α₀/Wᵖ + α₁` via `scipy.optimize.curve_fit` on (W, WMAPE) pairs across all forecasted buses. Use log-log space for the fit to give equal weight to small and large buses.
4. Report the four parameters with bootstrap confidence intervals (bootstrap over buses, 1,000 resamples):
   - **p** — expected range 0.88–1.07 from L29's results across SARMA/SVR/FFNN. If our LightGBM yields p outside this range, that is the headline finding.
   - **α₀** — reducible-error coefficient. L29's PG&E hour-ahead results gave α₀ ≈ 50; substantial deviation is reportable.
   - **α₁** — irreducible-error asymptote at large W. L29 reports α₁ ∈ [1.19, 2.13] across models for hour-ahead PG&E. Our day-ahead horizon should yield somewhat larger α₁; the value is the diagnostic quantity for our model's quality at the "easy" end of the scale.
   - **W★** — critical load above which further aggregation does not help. Solve `α₀/W★ᵖ = α₁` for W★ given the fitted parameters. L29's hour-ahead results yielded W★ ∈ [2.2, 72] MWh across models for PG&E.
5. Overlay our (W, WMAPE) scatter and the fitted curve on a single log-log plot — this is the report figure.
6. State explicitly whether our parameters are within order-of-magnitude of L29's PG&E results, and if not, discuss the climate / load-mix / horizon / scale differences that could account for the deviation.

**Forecast-horizon note (per L29-C):** Report fits separately for next-day and next-month horizons. L29's hour-ahead and day-ahead results differ substantially in W★ (day-ahead W★ ≈ 85 kWh vs hour-ahead W★ ≈ 2.2–72 MWh for the best models). Our next-day forecast is closest to L29's day-ahead regime; our next-month forecast is essentially out of L29's tested range — fitting it is exploratory.

**Scaling-axis note (per L29-E):** Use mean pd in MW (or MWh per hour, equivalently) as the x-axis, not bus count. L29 Section 5.4 establishes that energy is the canonical scaling quantity across residential/SMB/feeder/substation regimes.

**Cluster refinement (per Q6 linkage):** If Q6 produces sector clusters (residential / commercial / industrial-dominated diurnal shapes), fit the scaling law separately within each cluster. L29 found the law is robust across residential/SMB and coastal/inland subpopulations when scaled by kWh; if our ERCOT clusters produce different α₁ values, that's evidence sector composition affects the forecastability ceiling — a methodologically interesting result linking Q4 / Q8 to Q6.

**Honest acknowledgment of the rate-of-confirmation argument:** We are using the same LightGBM-direct-bus model for Q4 stratification *and* the Q8 fit. If the model is systematically biased (e.g., underpredicts small buses by a constant ratio), the scaling-law fit would be biased the same way. The fit confirms the *functional form* L29 predicts; it does not confirm our model is correctly calibrated. The two questions to report together are: (i) does the functional form match? (Q8 fit quality); (ii) are the parameters in a defensible range? (Q8 parameter comparison to L29 / L31 / L40).

**What success looks like:** A log-log plot with a clean linear-plus-asymptote shape, fit parameters within order-of-magnitude of published values, and a single sentence in the abstract: "We confirm the Sevlian-Rajagopal scaling law on ERCOT transmission-bus data; our LightGBM forecaster yields α₁ = [our value], p = [our value], comparable to published results on US distribution, UK LV-feeders, and Portuguese substations."

**Required models:** Direct bus model (the same one used for Q4). No additional model fitting — Q8 is a post-processing analysis on Q4's outputs. Approximately 30–50 lines of additional code (curve fit + bootstrap + plotting).

## Q9 — Does our model systematically underpredict at peak hours, and if so, would a peak-weighted custom loss meaningfully improve accuracy at those times?

**Why it matters:** Standard MSE/RMSE-trained gradient-boosted models tend to regress toward the conditional mean and can underweight extreme values. For ERCOT in particular, peak hours (typically summer afternoons during AC-driven peak demand, and the Winter Storm Elliott tail event in December 2022) are operationally the *most consequential* hours to forecast correctly — under-forecasting peak demand causes blackout risk; over-forecasting wastes spinning reserve. L41-B (Mathew et al. 2024, DEWA) is the direct precedent: they document 0.5–5% improvement in peak accuracy by adding a multiplicative weight `(1 + w·p)` to the MSE loss in pre-identified peak (month, hour) bins, with peak bins identified data-driven via prominence-peak detection plus bivariate histogram. L40-F (Pinheiro et al. 2023, Haben adjusted p-norm error with p=4, w=3) is the evaluation-side counterpart: a metric that penalizes large errors much more than small errors and tolerates small timing displacements. Together, L40-F and L41-B form a published training/evaluation pair for peak-sensitive STLF. **For ERCOT specifically, this question carries additional weight because of F3 (Winter Storm Elliott is in our training data) and F2 (FWES +60% growth means summer-peak demand has shifted upward over the training period).** The literature does not yet document peak-handling specifically for transmission-bus-scale STLF on ERCOT data, so a positive result is a methodological contribution beyond simply matching L41's DEWA-feeder finding.

**Why this is a two-stage question:**
1. **Diagnostic stage (essentially free, mandatory):** Slice E1's hour-of-day and month stratified errors specifically at the peak bins. Diagnose whether peak errors are systematically *biased* (e.g., underprediction with E[ŷ - y | peak] < 0) versus merely *high-variance* (e.g., RMSE is high at peak but bias is zero). If the model is high-variance but unbiased at peak, peak-weighted training will not help; if it is biased, peak-weighted training is the indicated treatment.
2. **Treatment stage (conditional, moderate cost):** Only if the diagnostic shows systematic peak bias, implement L41-B's PWP custom loss for LightGBM and re-evaluate.

The diagnostic stage is mandatory because it speaks to model calibration; the treatment stage is conditional on the diagnostic finding.

**How to answer it (diagnostic stage):**
1. From the direct bus model's 2025 test predictions, identify the peak bins data-driven via L41-B's two-stage workflow: (a) per-zone prominence-peak detection on the 2022–2024 historical zone load profile using `scipy.signal.find_peaks` with prominence threshold tuned to capture the top ~5% of values; (b) bivariate histogram of (month, hour) for detected peaks per zone; (c) assign p=1 to the top-N peak bins per zone (N ≈ 10–20 (month, hour) combinations per zone).
2. Compute, at peak bins versus non-peak bins, both: (i) mean signed error `E[ŷ - y]` (the bias diagnostic) and (ii) MAE/RMSE/WMAPE (the variance diagnostic). Compare bias-at-peak against bias-at-non-peak with a one-sample t-test on the per-bus mean signed error.
3. Additionally, compute the Haben adjusted p-norm error (APN with p=4, w=3 half-hours) on the test set per zone, alongside MAE/RMSE/WMAPE. If APN is dramatically worse than MAE/RMSE relative to typical ratios, peak handling is a real failure mode worth treating.
4. Stratify the diagnostic separately for: summer-peak hours (June–September, hours 14–18 for ERCOT typical), Winter Storm Elliott days (Dec 22–25, 2022 in training data), and FWES vs other zones (the structural-growth zone may have different peak dynamics).

**How to answer it (treatment stage, conditional on diagnostic showing peak bias):**
1. Implement L41-B's PWP custom loss for LightGBM. LightGBM custom objectives require returning `grad` and `hess` vectors; the PWP-MSE custom objective is straightforward — for the squared-error loss `L(y, ŷ) = (ŷ - y)² · (1 + w·p)`, the gradient is `2(ŷ - y)(1 + w·p)` and the Hessian is `2(1 + w·p)`. Both are simply the standard MSE gradient/Hessian scaled by `(1 + w·p)`. This is a ~10-line custom objective.
2. Tune `w` via cross-validation on the 2024 holdout split, with `w ∈ {0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10}`. Per L41, larger w increases peak accuracy at the cost of off-peak accuracy; find the sweet spot.
3. Compare the PWP-trained model against the baseline MSE-trained model on three metrics: aggregate WMAPE (should not worsen substantially), peak-bin MAE and APN (should improve), and aggregate-zone-sum RMSE (sanity check that peak improvements don't break zone aggregation).

**What we already know:** L41-B reports 0.5–5% peak-accuracy improvement at Dubai feeders. L40-F's APN measure is the published evaluation-side counterpart. The combined L40-F + L41-B framework is the most direct published methodology for this question.

**Failure modes to watch for:**
- **Peak bins defined too narrowly** (e.g., only the global peak hour each year) leave too few samples for training. Per L41 the prominence-peak approach yields ~5% of timestamps as peak bins, which is a reasonable training-data fraction.
- **Aggregate accuracy regression** is the main risk: heavily up-weighting peak samples can degrade off-peak accuracy more than it improves peak. The `w ∈ {0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10}` sweep makes this tradeoff explicit and reportable.
- **Test-set leakage via peak-bin identification:** the peak bins must be identified using *training-window* data only (2022–2024), not the 2025 test window. Document this explicitly to avoid the standard "look-ahead" pitfall.

**Failure-to-act argument:** Even if the diagnostic shows that our model is *not* systematically biased at peak hours, the diagnostic itself is valuable to report — it speaks to model calibration and is part of the standard E1 stratified evaluation. So Q9 is not "do something exotic"; it is "do an additional calibration diagnostic that the literature has shown is operationally important, and treat it only if needed."

**Required models:** Diagnostic stage: no additional model — analysis on existing Q4/E1 predictions. Treatment stage (conditional): one additional LightGBM model with custom PWP-MSE objective, with hyperparameter sweep over `w`.

**"Does adding weather features improve accuracy?" — CONDITIONAL, see Q10 above.**

*Defensive citation for the no-weather choice (added after L24 review, strengthened after L28, L31, L32, L34, L36, L41 reviews, extended after L12 anchor-tier rewrite from PDF):* We now have **nine independent peer-reviewed pieces of evidence** spanning a wide spectrum of weather-load correlation outcomes — from near-zero (FWES industrial, some 50Hertz substations) to very high (Dubai AC-dominated feeders) — that together support the heterogeneity-spectrum framing: weather-load correlation is highly climate-and-load-mix dependent, and ERCOT's diversified 8-zone footprint sits closer to the low-correlation end of the spectrum where a uniform weather feature would be more dilutive than additive. The nine pieces span four argument-types: "weather doesn't help on this dataset" (L24-A, L28-C, L31-B, L32-D), "weather absence is empirically normal" (L21), "weather effect heterogeneity makes uniform weather features problematic" (L34-A, L41-E), "bus-to-bus correlation dominates bus-to-weather correlation" (L36-A — strongest single piece), and "lag features dominate even when temperature is available" (L12-C — new middle-of-spectrum data point):
1. **L24-A (Safdarian et al. 2023):** On the same eight ERCOT weather zones over 12 years of historical data, Far West (= FWES) has near-zero temperature-load correlation across all four temperature partitions (peak |r|=0.17). The most ERCOT-specific challenge in our work — FWES structural growth — happens to be in the one zone where temperature would not have helped forecasting regardless.
2. **L28-C (Panapakidis et al. 2020, IET GTD):** On four Greek island bus loads with 4 years of data, adding mean daily temperature to a load-only feature set made forecasts *worse* across all three clustering algorithms and all six FFNN configurations. Case 1 (load only) MAPE 3.99–9.45%; Case 2 (load + temperature) MAPE 4.16–9.23%. Authors attribute to overfitting on small dataset. Even when weather data is available, including it does not guarantee accuracy improvement.
3. **L21 (Habib et al. 2026 survey):** Figure 8 documents that 22% (9 of 40) of STLF studies surveyed omitted weather features, and that historical lag features were universal (40/40). Our weather-free, lag-feature-driven approach is within empirical norms.
4. **L31-B (Haben et al. 2021, *Applied Energy* review, citing Haben et al. 2019):** UK LV-feeder forecasting study found "lack of influence of temperature on the forecast accuracy" at the low-voltage distribution-feeder level. This extends the no-weather-defense to a fourth scale (UK LV-feeder) and confirms the pattern crosses the Atlantic.
5. **L32-D (Nabavi et al. 2024):** Feature importance analysis on national-scale Iranian and German load shows temperature contributes <10% relative importance compared to lag features (importance score 100). At national aggregation, weather averages out — adds mild reinforcement at a different scale, although the scale gap (national vs. bus) makes this the weakest of the five pieces sharing the "weather doesn't help" framing.
6. **L34-A (Schröter et al. 2020, *Energies*, German 50Hertz TSO):** Pearson correlations between substation-specific weather data and historical load, across 56 transmission substations, span **r = 0.016 to 0.498 for temperature, r = 0.064 to 0.609 for global radiation, and r = 0.0152 to 0.819 for wind speed**. The **heterogeneity argument**: at bus/substation scale, weather effects are spatially heterogeneous enough that a single weather feature is not just unhelpful — it is potentially actively misleading. To use weather correctly at our 15,000-bus ERCOT scale would require *bus-specific weather joins*, which is a substantially larger data engineering scope than the assignment supports.
7. **L36-A (Chen et al. 2025, *Electric Power Systems Research*, Australian Essential Energy bus data) — STRONGEST SINGLE PIECE:** Pearson correlation analysis on 16 buses across 2 supply areas in Australia directly compares bus-to-weather PCC against bus-to-bus PCC on the same dataset. Bus-to-weather: mean |PCC| peaks at 0.29 (T2m), maximum individual r = 0.46. Bus-to-bus within the same supply area: mean |PCC| routinely exceeds 0.6-0.8, with values up to 0.94-0.97. **Direct quote: "the correlation between a bus and the remaining buses within the same supply area is far more significant than its correlation with meteorological features."** This is the strongest single piece because Chen et al. compute both axes on the same data and explicitly state the dominance. **Methodologically, the implication is that historical lag features from the target bus and information sharing across similar buses (via L5's Grouped-Global-Bus framework) should capture more predictive signal than weather features ever could** — at least at supply-area bus scale, which is the closest analog in the literature to our ERCOT transmission-bus problem.
8. **L41-E (Mathew et al. 2024, *Electric Power Systems Research*, Dubai DEWA feeder data) — HONEST COUNTER-EVIDENCE AT FACE VALUE, RESOLVED AS HIGH-END OF HETEROGENEITY SPECTRUM:** Pearson r = 0.75 between temperature and load at Dubai feeders, "in all feeders, we have observed a high correlation between load and temperature." This is the **highest weather-load correlation in our review**, dramatically above L36-A's max r=0.46 and L34-A's substation range. At face value, L41-E is counter-evidence to the no-weather defense. **Resolution via climate/load-mix caveat:** Dubai is summer-AC-dominated with no winter heating (temperature never drops below 16°C); AC load makes temperature a near-physical predictor for this specific climate and load mix. The closest ERCOT analog — FWES summer industrial cooling — was documented by L24-A to have *near-zero* temperature correlation because industrial process load dominates over weather-sensitive load. **The cumulative picture across L24-A + L34-A + L36-A + L41-E is that weather-load correlation spans r = 0.016 to r = 0.75 across reviewed papers**, depending on climate and load mix. ERCOT's diversified 8-zone footprint with substantial FWES industrial load and varied residential/commercial mixes sits closer to the low-correlation end of this spectrum, where a uniform weather feature across the footprint would be more dilutive than additive.
9. **L12-C (Shiblee & Koukaras 2025, *Energies*, Greek national STLF) — MIDDLE-OF-SPECTRUM DATA POINT, TEMPERATURE AVAILABLE BUT MID-RANK IN FEATURE IMPORTANCE:** L12 Section 2.3, Figure 1 ranks LightGBM feature importance on 10 years of Greek national hourly load data: **hour > lag_1 > lag_24 > rolling_mean_3 > temperature > lag_168 > month > weekday > year > day > humidity > is_holiday**. Two findings critical to our no-weather defense: (i) **temporal lag features (lag_1, lag_24, lag_168) and the hour-of-day calendar feature dominate**, with the top 4 features all being temporal/lag-derived — empirical confirmation that lag-based features are the workhorses of STLF as L32-B/L32-C/L37-A documented; (ii) **temperature is mid-rank — below the dominant lag features but above month/weekday calendar features** — and (iii) **humidity is near-bottom and holidays are essentially noise**, despite Greece having a Mediterranean climate where temperature should physically matter for heating/cooling load. L12 is a **middle-of-spectrum data point**: temperature is *available* on a climate that is more weather-sensitive than ERCOT-FWES industrial load (Mediterranean residential AC + winter heating) yet *substantially less* weather-sensitive than Dubai DEWA (r=0.75) — and even there, temperature contributes meaningfully but does not dominate. **For our heterogeneity-spectrum framing, L12-C strengthens the defense by filling in the middle ground:** the cumulative evidence is now L24-A (near-zero r=0.017, US industrial) → L34-A (r=0.016 to 0.498 heterogeneous, German transmission-substation) → L36-A (mean |r|=0.29, max 0.46, Australian bus, dominated by bus-bus correlation) → L12-C (mid-rank LightGBM importance, Greek national Mediterranean) → L41-E (r=0.75, Dubai AC-dominated). **Even in the middle of the climate-and-load-mix spectrum (Greek Mediterranean with mixed residential/commercial), lag features dominate over temperature in a LightGBM model identical in spirit to what we plan to use.** L12 is the closest method-family analog to our pipeline in our entire review, and it produces a feature-importance ranking that explicitly supports our F1 emphasis on lag features over weather features.

The nine pieces span seven distinct grid types (US distribution L24, Greek island L28, UK LV-feeder L31, two national grids L32, German transmission-substation L34, Australian sub-transmission L36, Greek national L12, Dubai distribution L41) and six problem framings (zonal STLF, bus STLF, national STLF, transmission-substation STLF, medium-term feeder forecasting, LightGBM-vs-DL benchmark), plus the Survey-of-Forecasting-Practice (L21) baseline. **In residential/commercial-dominated zones (Coast, North, South, etc.), weather absence remains a real but bounded methodological limitation we should acknowledge honestly in the report.** But the nine-piece evidence cluster (L24-A + L28-C + L21 + L31-B + L32-D + L34-A + L36-A + L41-E + L12-C) — interpreted through the heterogeneity-spectrum framing — supports our M0 / scoping decision as defensible, not as a corner cut. **L36-A is the strongest single piece** because it directly compares bus-to-weather against bus-to-bus correlation on the same dataset and states the dominance explicitly. **L41-E is honest counter-evidence** that resolves into the heterogeneity argument: weather correlation depends critically on climate and load mix, and ERCOT's diversified footprint sits closer to the low-correlation end of the spectrum. **L12-C is the closest method-family analog in our review (LightGBM + lag features + calendar) and shows lag features dominate temperature even on a Mediterranean dataset with temperature available.** Our choice to lean on lag features and bus-similarity rather than weather features is defensible exactly because of this heterogeneity — a uniform weather feature would harmonize across heterogeneous bus-level coupling regimes, and even on a *favorable* (single-station, single-climate, weather-data-available) dataset like L12's Greek national load, lag features still dominate.

**"Does a deep learning model outperform LightGBM?"**
At 15,000 buses, training and evaluating a deep learning model within assignment time constraints is not realistic. LightGBM is the appropriate choice for this scale and is defensible on four grounds: (1) **methodological benchmark evidence** — **L12-A (Shiblee & Koukaras 2025, *Energies*, our anchor-tier citation after PDF re-read) is now THE primary citation in our methodological-benchmark stack**: six metrics × five models × matched conditions on 10 years of Greek national hourly load data (LightGBM test MAE 69.12 / RMSE 101.67 / MAPE 1.20% / R² 0.9942 dominates LSTM 87.26/118.68/1.53%/0.9921, GRU 120.64/158.80/2.03%/0.9859, CNN 148.05/194.10/2.59%/0.9789, and hybrid CNN-LSTM 158.23/258.05/2.43%/0.9628 across every metric in every train/validation/test set, beating LSTM by 21% MAE / 14% RMSE / 22% MAPE; the cleanest LightGBM-vs-DL head-to-head in our review). **L12-B (LightGBM beats the operational ENTSO-E utility forecast)**: same paper, Table 6 — LightGBM MAE 69.12 / MAPE 1.20% vs ENTSO-E operational MAE 133.31 / MAPE 2.33% / R² 0.9813, an approximately 48% improvement over the actual production utility forecast that European grid operations rely on. The strongest single-paper existence-proof in our review that a properly-engineered LightGBM model can outperform real-world production-grade utility STLF, not just research benchmarks. **L12-D (LightGBM-over-XGBoost on memory grounds)**: explicit justification from L12 Section 3.6: "We used LightGBM as the boosted-tree baseline (chosen over XGBoost for faster, more memory-efficient training on the 10-year dataset)" — direct precedent for our LightGBM-over-XGBoost choice at our 525M-row scale (15,000 buses × 4 years × 8760 hours), citing LightGBM's histogram-based feature bundling. **L5-E (Triebe et al. 2025, our anchor-tier citation after PDF re-read) adds the most nuanced piece of evidence in the entire stack:** hits-GAM beats XGBoost at utility-aggregate level on absolute metrics (RMSE 105.41 vs 144.92 / MAE 78.22 vs 108.61 / MAPE 4.21% vs 5.53% — a 24% MAPE reduction) **but TIES on scaled metrics (MASE 0.58 vs 0.59 / MSSE 0.36 vs 0.37)**. **Honest reading of L5-E: hits-GAM does not dominate XGBoost; it wins on absolute metrics, ties on scaled metrics, and the gain comes substantially from interpretability and uncertainty quantification (Quantile Regression + Pinball Loss) rather than raw accuracy.** This is a more nuanced framing than the original L5 entry suggested — LightGBM (which is closer in spirit to XGBoost than to hits-GAM) is competitive with hits-GAM on the scaled metrics that matter for cross-series comparison. **L41-A (Mathew et al. 2024, DEWA)** provides the cleanest XGBoost-vs-LSTM head-to-head in our review: on 5 Dubai feeders, XGBoost wins R² on 4 of 5 (0.92–0.95), and while LSTM marginally wins RMSE/MAPE on most feeders, LSTM "is very data-specific, requiring hyperparameter tuning for each feeder, and if the data is less, the LSTM model fails," takes ~78× longer to train (366.74 s vs 4.68 s), and requires GPU vs CPU. Grinsztajn et al. (2022, cited in L5 reference [37]) provides the more general claim that tree-based models outperform deep learning on tabular data. **L12-E adds an indirect-support data point**: hybrid CNN-LSTM in L12 Table 3 actively *worsens* performance vs either component alone (Hybrid test MAE 158.23 vs CNN 148.05 vs LSTM 87.26 vs LightGBM 69.12), demonstrating that architectural complexity is not progress within the DL family itself. (2) **practitioner-philosophy evidence** — L34-C (Schröter et al. 2020) explicitly articulates the production-TSO operational position: "Due to the criticality of the subsequent processes, stable and simple calculation methods are primarily used for system management" (Section 3.2.2), and explicitly justifies rejecting dynamic combination on stability grounds; **L40-E (Pinheiro et al. 2023)** adds a fourth practitioner-philosophy citation: applicability + interpretability + reproducibility are co-equal with accuracy as evaluation criteria for production STLF systems, "a model containing that variable is not applicable, no matter how well that variable would improve the predictions" if the utility cannot operationally obtain it; **L41-G (Mathew et al. 2024, DEWA)** adds a fifth practitioner-philosophy citation with the most directly transferable industrial-utility context: "XGBoost can also deal with missing data, which reduces the effort in preprocessing techniques and it is robust to outliers... we can execute the XGBoost model on a normal CPU, whereas we need GPUs to run the LSTM model. XGBoost model is more explainable than the deep learning models like LSTM" — these are exactly the deployment constraints an ERCOT pipeline would face; (3) **scale evidence** — even Schröter et al.'s 56-substation problem used multiple linear regression + a small neural network combined statically, not deep learning, in a real industrial operational context, and Pinheiro et al.'s 96,989-substation deployment used GAM, not deep learning, and Mathew et al.'s DEWA feeder forecasting selected XGBoost over LSTM explicitly on data-availability and computational-cost grounds, **and L5's MISO ~100-bus problem uses hits-GAM (an interpretable GAM with neural-network components) rather than black-box deep learning** — the methodological anchor paper itself rejects pure DL in favor of an interpretable framework that scales sub-linearly with bus count. Our 15,000-bus problem at greater scale supports the same conclusion *a fortiori*; (4) **simple-method-near-ties-complex-method evidence at bus scale** — L36-B (Chen et al. 2025) shows that TCN ranks 2nd of 10 baselines on day-ahead bus load forecasting in two Australian supply areas, only 4.6%-6.4% worse RMSE than the proposed FC-STGAT, while Transformer and Informer are 50%+ worse. This is empirical evidence at the closest-scale-match in our review (bus-level day-ahead forecasting on real data) that simple temporal architectures (TCN) compete strongly with sophisticated GNN+attention architectures, and substantially outperform plain attention-based methods. **Honest acknowledgments:** **L37-D (Ferreira et al. 2025):** TFT on an aggregated NZ subsystem achieves 0.94% MAPE vs. older ARTMAP-family ANN methods at 2.12%-2.91% MAPE — modern DL does improve over older ANN baselines. But L37 does not test TFT against LightGBM, and its very-high-aggregation setting (peak ~310 GW system load) is far from our bus-level problem. **L40-B (Pinheiro et al. 2023) methodological caveat:** at Portuguese national scale with carefully engineered domain-knowledge features (calendar variables, lagged loads, day-type expansion, GAM-spline temperature interactions), GAMLF-SL-M3 achieves 191 MW RMSE while XGBoost (GBMLF-SL) with exhaustive hyperparameter grid search achieves only 199 MW RMSE — **gradient boosting does not unconditionally win** when domain features are well-designed. **L5-E (added after L5 anchor-tier rewrite from PDF) adds analogous nuance at MISO utility-aggregate scale:** hits-GAM beats XGBoost on absolute metrics but ties on scaled metrics; the case for hits-GAM is interpretability + uncertainty quantification, not raw accuracy. Both L40-B and L5-E *complicate* the L12 LightGBM-over-DL framing without overturning it: at our bus-level scale, the L29 scaling law predicts much larger relative errors than the national-aggregated regime where L40 operates and the utility-aggregate regime where L5 operates, and LightGBM's tree splits should handle the lag-feature-dominated structure that L32-B/L32-C/L37-A/L12-C document as standard at bus scale. The takeaway is that **method-family choice matters less than feature engineering**, which is consistent with our F1/F2 emphasis but worth honest acknowledgment that LightGBM is not unconditionally the strongest choice — it is the strongest choice given (i) our scale per L29's scaling law, (ii) our feature set (lag features + calendar + structural growth trend), (iii) the L12 + L34-C + L36-B + L40-E + L41-G practitioner-philosophy stack, (iv) the L12-D published memory-efficiency justification for LightGBM-over-XGBoost at multi-year hourly scale, and (v) deployment-feasibility within assignment time constraints.

**"Does clustering buses improve accuracy via separate models per cluster?"**
This is worth investigating but only after the global model baseline is working. If time permits, compare Global-Bus vs Grouped-Global-Bus (L5 Triebe et al. framework). Otherwise it belongs in the literature backlog. Q6 tests the weaker version (cluster as feature in global model), which is much cheaper.

**"Is the next-month forecast better or worse than next-day?"**
Both horizons are required by the assignment. Report both. But we are not designing separate models for the comparison — the same model architecture handles both by adjusting the forecast horizon and the lag constraints.

**"Does a learnable adjacency matrix GCN (L20 LASTGCN) outperform LightGBM?"**
At 15,000 buses, a learnable 15,000×15,000 adjacency matrix is 225M parameters in the adjacency representation alone, plus the GCN model parameters on top. Computationally intractable within assignment constraints. L20 authors explicitly acknowledge scale as a limitation of their approach.
