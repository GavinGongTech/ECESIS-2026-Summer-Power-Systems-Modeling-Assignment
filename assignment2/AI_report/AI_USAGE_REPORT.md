## 1. Scope of AI use, by project phase

I broke the project into five phases. AI was used differently in each.

### Phase 1 — Initial EDA and forecastable-universe definition (notebook 01)

**AI usage: moderate.** I built the data audit largely by myself, but used Claude to pressure-test the funnel logic. The 18,643 → 4,208 funnel went through several revisions before settling on the five-filter design (ISOLATED removal, GEN removal, SWING removal, always-null LOAD removal, ≥50% extended-absence filter). The 50% extended-absence threshold specifically came from a back-and-forth where I noticed a percentile cliff in the per-bus null distribution (75th percentile 11.4%, 90th percentile 86.5%) and Claude helped me articulate this as a defensible empirical-cliff justification rather than a fixed-threshold convention.

**Specific decisions where AI input mattered:** the three-tier-vs-two-tier imputation choice (Triebe et al. use a two-tier recipe; my lifecycle-dominated null patterns forced a third tier with row-dropping rather than imputation across multi-month gaps).

### Phase 2 — Literature review (six backlog files, ~41 papers)

**AI usage: heavy and the most distinctive part of this project.** I produced six structured markdown files in `assignment2/backlogs/`:

| File | Lines | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| `BACKLOG_PRIMARY.md` | ~300 | Original implementation backlog from EDA findings (items M1-M2, D1-D4, B1, F1-F3, E1-E3) |
| `BACKLOG_PRIMARY_REFINEMENTS.md` | ~400 | Refinements driven by the literature review; stage 1/1.5/2/3 strategy; items S1 and G1 |
| `BACKLOG_LITERATURE.md` | ~1,700 | Full PhD-level analyses of 41 peer-reviewed papers in bus-level STLF |
| `BACKLOG_REFERENCE_TRACKER.md` | ~250 | Citation-graph analysis identifying the Tao Hong UNC-Charlotte hub and Stanford Rajagopal cluster |
| `BIG_QUESTIONS.md` | ~600 | Research questions Q1-Q10 driving the experimental matrix |
| `CONSOLIDATION.md` | ~500 | Synthesis organizing literature into 6 actionable parts |

The literature review proceeded one paper at a time. For each paper I uploaded the PDF, Claude extracted text, and we co-authored a 100-300 word structured analysis with relevance score (1-10), specific extractable findings labeled with internal codes (e.g., "L5-B", "L24-A"), and explicit connections to my pipeline decisions. The anchor papers — Triebe et al. 2025 (MISO), Sevlian & Rajagopal 2018 (scaling law), Wickramasuriya et al. 2019 (MinT), Schäfer & Strimmer 2005 (shrinkage), Hong & Fan 2016 (probabilistic review), Schröter et al. 2020 (substation-level heterogeneity), Safdarian et al. 2023 (ERCOT zone correlations), Panapakidis et al. 2020 (Greek island weather ablation) — were analyzed at deeper depth.

**What this got me that I could not have produced alone in the available time:** the seven-piece evidence cluster supporting the regime-dependent weather contribution prediction (Section 3.4 of the report). That argument structure — anchored across four argument-types from peer-reviewed sources — is what allowed me to (a) commit confidently to building both weather-augmented and no-weather pipelines, (b) defend the methodological choice in writing, and (c) interpret the empirical results in terms that the literature had already predicted.

### Phase 3 — Notebook implementation (notebooks 02-08, including 02w, 04b, 05b, 06b)

**AI usage: light to moderate.** All code was verified by me. AI was used for:

- **Code review** of specific functions where I was uncertain (the MinT projection matrix, the bus-share renormalization with cold-start fallback, the strict-warm-start hyperparameter inheritance pattern).
- **Bug diagnosis** when a result looked anomalous (the cold-start 220% WMAPE for zone-direct in notebook 06 cell 6, the global-bus next-month α ≈ 0.28 scaling-law anomaly).
- **Methodological sounding** for non-obvious choices: which sNaïve baselines to construct, how to handle the val→test transfer, whether to estimate MinT covariance on validation residuals (Option Z, the rigorous choice) or on test residuals (Option Y, which I ultimately adopted due to scope constraints), and how to specify the strict-warm-start ablation in notebooks 04b and 05b.

I deliberately did *not* ask AI to write the implementation autonomously. Every notebook was authored cell-by-cell, with markdown observations that I wrote in response to each cell's output. The conversation logs reflect this: I paste empirical outputs (e.g., "MinT-shrink reduces nextday bus RMSE by 32% on the reconciled subset") and Claude helps me interpret them, not generate them.

### Phase 4 — Evaluation and the surprising findings (notebooks 06 and 06b)

**AI usage: moderate.** The most methodologically interesting AI-assisted moment came in notebook 06b cell 10 (the MinT deep-dive). The cell-7 headline finding — "MinT-shrink wins zone-level decisively, and is competitive at bus level" — was real but mechanistically concentrated. The per-bus inspection revealed that a single 345 kV transmission bus (`CHLDDATA_345KV_1`) drives most of the aggregate gain, and 35-45% of reconciled buses are *hurt* by reconciliation. The honest revised framing in the report — concentrated wins rather than uniform improvement — emerged from Claude pressing me on whether the aggregate metric story was the full story. I had the numbers; I needed help seeing that they told a more nuanced story than the headline suggested.

A second similar moment: the architectural-disagreement finding (zone-direct wins zone-level, global-bus wins bus-level — no universal winner). I had run both architectures and had the numbers, but the framing of this as "the model an operator should use depends on what aggregation level the operator cares about" — explicitly answering the assignment's literal question — came out of a conversation where Claude pointed out that I was about to bury the headline.

### Phase 5 — Report writing (this report and the main IEEEtran PDF)

**AI usage: heavy.** I drafted the report iteratively across two conversations totaling roughly 50,000 words of dialogue. The workflow:

1. I uploaded notebook PDFs (01, 02w) and the assignment specification.
2. I provided cell-level markdown outputs from notebooks 06 and 06b as text in chat (the empirical results that drive every Section 5 claim).
3. Claude drafted each report section (1-7) in LaTeX directly, anchoring every empirical claim to a specific cell output.
4. I reviewed each section, pushed back on specific framings, and requested revisions.
5. After all seven sections were drafted, I requested a thorough audit pass anchoring on Section 5 (the empirical-results section) as the canonical source of truth. This pass identified 24 issues across the seven sections (stale weather framings from before notebook 06 results were in hand, off-by-one contribution counts, internal-backlog literature labels leaking into the body, broken LaTeX cross-references, and so on) which were fixed systematically.

The final report — 1,905 lines of LaTeX, 12,215 words of body text, 20 BibTeX entries — is the product of this iterative drafting. Substantively, every methodological commitment and every empirical claim is mine; Claude's contribution was drafting, structuring, cross-referencing, and pushing back when a claim was overstated or under-defended.

## 3. What I did *not* use AI for

A balanced disclosure requires saying what AI was *not* used for, both because the assignment asks for honesty about AI usage and because some recruiters will read this report with a healthy skepticism.

- **No code was generated autonomously.** Every line of every notebook, every preprocessing function, every model training loop, every evaluation function, every plotting routine was verified by me. I do not have any AI-generated `*.py` or `*.ipynb` files in this repository without proper justification, supervision. No code was written fully by AI and I created and ran the processes on the jupyter notebooks myself.
- **No empirical results were generated by AI.** Every WMAPE, RMSE, MAE number in the report comes from my evaluation code running on my machine against the actual 2025 test set. The 7.51% bus-level next-day WMAPE for global-bus-weather, the 4.10% zone-level next-day WMAPE for zone-direct-weather, the −193.93 MW per-bus RMSE reduction at CHLDDATA-345KV-1 under MinT-shrink, the 35-45% bus-hurt rate — these are all from my notebooks, not AI inference.
- **No literature was fabricated.** Where I cite a paper, I read the paper. Where the bibliography contains an entry I could not verify against the original PDF (the six Tier 3 entries flagged in `references.bib`), the report explicitly notes the verification status.
- **No experimental decision was delegated.** When Claude proposed a methodological alternative (e.g., "you could implement Grouped-Global-Bus clustering"), I evaluated it on its merits and either committed it to the pipeline, deferred it to future work (Section 6), or rejected it.
- **No final commit, push, or repository action was performed by AI.** All `git` operations were executed by me, on my machine, with my credentials.

## 4. Limitations and honesty disclosures

Three limitations of the AI-assisted workflow that a recruiter should know about:

**4.1 Knowledge cutoff.** Claude's training data has a cutoff (Sonnet 4.6 at January 2025; Opus 4.7 at January 2026). For the most recent papers in the literature review (Triebe et al. 2025, Mathew et al. 2024), I had to upload the PDFs and have Claude extract the text rather than rely on prior knowledge. Where I could not upload the PDF, the bibliography entry is flagged as needing verification.

**4.2 Citation hallucination risk.** Large language models are known to fabricate plausible-looking but non-existent citations. I mitigated this in two ways: (a) every cited paper was either uploaded to the conversation in full or referenced by me from my own collection, and (b) the final pre-submission audit pass aggressively removed two BibTeX entries (`chen2025fcstgat`, `habib2026`) where I could not confidently attribute first author. Six remaining Tier 3 entries are flagged in `references.bib` for the recruiter to verify against the original PDFs if needed.

**4.3 Drafting bias toward conventional academic prose.** The report's tone is methodologically careful and somewhat formal, which is a feature of AI-drafted academic writing in general. I reviewed and revised the language throughout to ensure it reads as my voice rather than a generic LLM voice. Specific stylistic commitments — the willingness to surface the concentrated-wins finding prominently, the explicit honesty disclosures in Section 6, the operational recommendation matrix in Section 5.2 — are mine.

## 5. Why I think this AI-usage pattern is the right pattern for ECESIS work

Quantitative research in energy markets — the work ECESIS does — depends on three things that AI tools genuinely amplify: (1) the ability to absorb and synthesize a large research literature quickly, (2) the ability to pressure-test methodological commitments before they become embedded in code, and (3) the ability to produce well-organized written deliverables that survive scrutiny by senior reviewers. AI tools used as a *research multiplier* — used to read more papers, audit more design choices, and produce cleaner writing — are a legitimate productivity tool that responsible practitioners are using throughout the financial-modeling industry. AI tools used as a *substitute for thinking* — used to write code one cannot read, to claim results one did not generate, or to fabricate citations one did not verify — would be a disqualifying problem. This report exists to make the distinction explicit, and to give the recruiter a clear way to verify which pattern this work follows. The pattern, I hope this report demonstrates, is the first.
